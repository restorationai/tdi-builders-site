## What does new home construction in Roseville actually cost?

A new custom home in Roseville runs $250 to $400 per square foot for construction costs alone, not counting land, permits, utility hookups, or landscaping. A 2,000-square-foot home lands between $500,000 and $800,000 in hard construction costs. Production builders in planned communities like Fiddyment Farm can come in lower because they're building the same floor plan repeatedly, but a custom or semi-custom build on your own lot will sit in that range.

Here's what drives the spread:

- **Foundation type**: A post-tension slab (standard in Roseville's expansive clay soils) costs more than a simple poured slab. A raised foundation adds more.
- **Roof pitch and complexity**: A simple 4:12 gable roof is far cheaper to frame than a hip roof with multiple valleys.
- **Finish level**: Builder-grade cabinets, LVP flooring, and stock fixtures land at the low end. Custom cabinetry, engineered hardwood, and stone countertops push toward the top.
- **Energy code compliance**: California's Title 24 requires solar-ready or solar-installed homes, high-efficiency HVAC, and specific insulation R-values. These are not optional line items.
- **Site conditions**: Rocky ground, poor drainage, or a steeply sloped lot in the Granite Bay-adjacent areas of east Roseville can add $20,000 to $60,000 in site prep.

Budget separately for permits ($15,000 to $30,000+ depending on square footage and fees), utility connections ($10,000 to $25,000 for water, sewer, gas, and electric), and landscaping ($15,000 to $50,000 for a finished yard).

## How does the Roseville permitting process work?

The City of Roseville processes residential building permits through its Development Services Center on Vernon Street. For a new single-family home, you'll submit architectural plans, structural calculations, a soils report (required for most Roseville lots due to expansive clay), Title 24 energy calculations, and a grading plan if the lot has significant slope.

Plan check typically takes 6 to 10 weeks for an initial review. Corrections are common on first submission. A complete permit package from an experienced contractor or architect reduces the round-trip time significantly. After permit issuance, you'll schedule inspections at each phase: foundation, framing, rough mechanical/electrical/plumbing, insulation, and final. The City of Roseville's inspection scheduling is online and generally books 2 to 5 days out, which is faster than many Sacramento-area jurisdictions.

If your lot is in a planned development with a homeowners association, add HOA architectural review to the timeline. Some West Roseville HOAs require their own approval before you can even submit to the city. That can add 4 to 8 weeks.

For a full breakdown of which projects require permits and why, the [City of Roseville's Building Division](https://www.roseville.ca.us/gov/departments/development_services/building) publishes its fee schedule and submittal requirements online. Our post on [permit requirements for home projects](/blog/do-you-need-a-permit/) also covers the general framework if you want context before your first contractor meeting.

## What does the construction timeline look like?

For a custom home in Roseville, plan on 10 to 18 months from signed contract to move-in. Here's a realistic phase breakdown:

**Pre-construction (2 to 4 months):** Design, engineering, soils report, permit submission, and plan check. This phase often surprises buyers because it happens before any physical work begins. Don't underestimate it.

**Site prep and foundation (3 to 6 weeks):** Grading, underground utilities, formwork, and slab pour. Post-tension slabs require a waiting period before stressing the cables and proceeding.

**Framing (4 to 8 weeks):** For a 2,000-square-foot home, a good framing crew frames the structure in 3 to 4 weeks. Roof sheathing and felt follow immediately. Framing inspection comes next.

**Rough mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (4 to 8 weeks):** HVAC ductwork, electrical rough-in, and plumbing rough-in happen simultaneously with multiple subcontractors on site. Coordination here is where a general contractor earns their fee.

**Insulation, drywall, and finishes (8 to 14 weeks):** Insulation inspection, drywall hang and tape, texture, paint, cabinet install, countertop template and install, tile, flooring, trim, fixtures, and appliances. This is the longest phase and the one most sensitive to material lead times.

**Final inspections and punch list (2 to 4 weeks):** City final inspection, utility energization, and your own walkthrough punch list.

Weather rarely stops work in Roseville the way it does in wetter climates, but the valley heat in July and August slows concrete work and can affect crew productivity on exposed framing.

## What should you look for in a Roseville new construction contractor?

A licensed general contractor is the right structure for a new home build. They hold the primary contract, pull the permits in their name, and are responsible for coordinating every subcontractor from foundation to finish. Under California law, contractors performing work over $500 must hold a valid [CSLB license](https://www.cslb.ca.gov). Verify any contractor's license status directly on the CSLB website before signing anything.

Beyond the license, look for:

- **Demonstrated local experience**: A contractor who has pulled permits in Roseville knows the city's plan check comments, the inspectors' expectations, and which subcontractors are reliable in the 916 market.
- **A clear written contract**: Scope of work, allowances for finishes, payment schedule tied to milestones, and a change-order process. Vague contracts are where cost overruns hide.
- **References from comparable projects**: A remodel contractor and a new construction contractor are not the same thing. Ask for references from ground-up builds, not just renovations.
- **Insurance documentation**: General liability and workers' compensation certificates, current and naming you as an additional insured.

If you're comparing bids and one comes back 20% lower than the others, ask what's excluded. Missing allowances, excluded permits, or a thinner subcontractor bench are common sources of low bids that don't stay low.
